AI companies don't have an awareness problem

Most AI startups think they need more awareness. They need less noise. Here is how trusted creators cut through, and how to tell which problem you have.

Salty QaziFounder, GRVTY

Every few weeks a founder tells me nobody knows their company exists. They have shipped a real product. They have a launch video, a waitlist and a few thousand followers. Their conclusion is that they need more awareness: more posts, more ads, more launches.

I think that diagnosis is wrong, and acting on it usually makes things worse.

AI companies don't have an awareness problem. They have a noise problem. Your buyers have heard of you, or of something that sounds exactly like you. They have seen a hundred posts this week about agents, copilots and models that will change everything. Your message is not failing to reach them. It reaches them, sits next to forty similar messages, and gets filed under hype.

Why more volume makes it worse

When you treat a noise problem as an awareness problem, you add volume. You post more. You buy more impressions. You sponsor a creator with a huge general tech audience and hand them a script full of claims.

Each of those moves adds to the static. Your audience is technical, or at least technically literate. They have learned to skim past anything that sounds like a pitch. Every generic post trains them a little more to ignore the next one, including yours.

The mechanism is simple. Attention in AI is not scarce because people are busy. It is scarce because people are defensive. They have been burned by demos that didn't hold up and launches that were mostly marketing. So they apply a filter, and the filter is tuned to reject exactly the kind of content that volume-driven marketing produces.

What actually gets through the filter

The thing that cuts through is trust. Specifically, a trusted creator who knows signal from noise, speaking to an audience that can tell the difference.

Think about how you decide which AI tools to try. You probably don't click on ads. You follow a handful of people who actually use these tools, who have been right before, and who are willing to say when something is bad. When one of them says a product is worth an afternoon, you give it an afternoon.

That is the whole model. A credible creator lends you their filter. Their audience trusts that filter because it has rejected things before. When your product passes through it, you get the benefit of every weak product they declined to cover.

This only works under three conditions:

  • The creator has real technical depth. They need to understand what your product does and where it sits next to the alternatives. If they can't explain it accurately, the audience notices.
  • The audience is literate. A creator with a large audience that can't tell a good product from a bad one gives you reach without trust.
  • The content is honest. The moment it reads like a script, the filter switches back on.

A quick diagnostic: noise or awareness

Before you spend another dollar on distribution, run through these questions. Be honest with the answers.

  1. When prospects find you, do they already know the category? If yes, awareness of the category is not your constraint.
  2. Can a stranger explain how your product differs from the three closest alternatives after reading your homepage? If not, you are adding to the noise.
  3. Do your best leads come from referrals and word of mouth rather than paid channels? That tells you trust converts and reach alone doesn't.
  4. Has your impression count gone up while conversion stayed flat? That is the clearest signal of a noise problem.
  5. When people mention you online, do they describe you accurately? If they repeat your tagline back but can't say what you do, your message is landing as noise.

If three or more of your answers point toward noise, more awareness spend will mostly buy you more static.

Pull, not push

The fix is to change direction. Push marketing puts your brand in front of people and hopes some of them care. Pull marketing puts the right voice in front of the right audience and lets curiosity do the rest.

We don't push brands in front of people. We use the right voices to pull them in.

In practice, pull looks like this. A creator who covers AI as their primary beat spends real time with your product. They find the part that is genuinely interesting to their audience, which is often not the part you lead with. They make content about that, in their own format, on the platform where their audience already pays attention. Viewers who care follow up on their own. They arrive with context, which means they behave very differently from someone who clicked an ad.

The best campaigns don't feel like campaigns. They feel like a person you trust telling you about something they found.

What this changes about your plan

If you accept the noise framing, a few practical decisions follow.

First, you stop measuring success by reach. Reach is the cheapest thing to buy and the least useful thing to have when your audience is filtering. You start measuring depth: who engaged, whether they understood, and what they did next.

Second, you spend more time on the story before you spend money on distribution. If your narrative sounds like everyone else's, no creator can save it. The narrative needs to earn trust before it asks for attention.

Third, you choose fewer, better voices. One creator whose audience is exactly your buyer is worth more than ten whose audiences are vaguely interested in AI.

Fourth, you give up some control. Trusted creators stay trusted because they say what they actually think. If you need every sentence approved, you are paying for an ad with a person's face on it, and the audience will treat it that way.

The signal in the static

Awareness is easy to buy. Trust has to be earned, and in AI it is the thing that actually moves the metrics you care about. That is the idea GRVTY is built on: a network of more than 100 AI creators, each covering AI as a primary vertical, matched to audiences that can tell signal from noise.
