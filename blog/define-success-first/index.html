Define success before the first brief

Most creator campaigns measure whatever is easy after the fact. Here is how to define success before the first brief and build attribution around it.

Salty QaziFounder, GRVTY

Here is how most creator campaigns end. The content goes live. A few weeks later, someone pulls a report. It shows impressions, views, engagement rate and maybe some clicks. Everyone looks at it and tries to decide whether it worked.

Nobody can, because nobody decided what working meant before it started.

This is the most avoidable failure in creator marketing. It is also the most common. The fix is boring: define success before the first brief is written, then build attribution around what actually matters to you.

Why after-the-fact measurement fails

When you measure after the fact, you measure what is available, not what matters. Platform dashboards give you views and engagement because those are easy to count. Those numbers become the story by default.

The problem is that they rarely connect to the outcomes a founder or marketing lead cares about. You care about trials, activated accounts, pipeline, retention, or a specific shift in who is signing up. A view count doesn't tell you any of that.

Worse, after-the-fact measurement invites motivated reasoning. If the numbers are good, the campaign worked. If they're bad, the campaign was about awareness anyway. You learn nothing either way.

Start with the business question

Before we write a brief, we ask one question: if this goes well, what will be different in ninety days?

The answer is usually specific once you push on it. Here are some examples of good answers, all hypothetical:

  • More developers on the free tier who make at least one API call in their first week.
  • More inbound demo requests from teams above a certain size.
  • A higher share of signups who come from a technical role rather than general interest.
  • More prospects who already understand the product when they reach a sales call.

Each of those implies different creators, different content and different measurement. A campaign built for API activation should look nothing like one built for enterprise demos. If you don't decide up front, you end up running a vague campaign that serves neither.

Pick one. Teams often want all four, and that is how campaigns end up built for nothing in particular. You can track secondary effects, but the brief, the creator selection and the report should all point at a single primary outcome. Everything else is context.

If you can't say what success looks like before the campaign, you won't recognize it after.

A framework for defining success

Here is the framework we use. It fits on one page, and it should be agreed before any creator is contacted.

  1. The outcome. One primary business result, stated in your own terms. Not a channel metric. Something your finance team would recognize.
  2. The leading indicators. Two or three signals that should move before the outcome does. For a developer tool, this might be docs traffic from creator-referred visitors, or the share of new signups who complete setup.
  3. The baseline. What those numbers look like today, without the campaign. Without a baseline, any number looks like progress.
  4. The time window. When you expect to see movement. Creator content has a long tail, especially on YouTube and in newsletters. Set the window to match the platforms you are using.
  5. The attribution method. How you will connect creator content to the outcome. This is where most teams get stuck, so it gets its own section below.
  6. The decision rule. What you will do if the numbers hit, miss, or land in between. Decide this now, while you are still objective.

Building attribution around it

Creator attribution is imperfect. People watch a video on one device, think about it for a week, and sign up from another. Anyone who promises perfect attribution is guessing.

That doesn't mean you give up. It means you combine methods so each one covers the others' blind spots:

  • Direct tracking. Unique links and codes per creator. They undercount, because many people never click. They are still the cleanest signal you have.
  • Self-reported attribution. A single open question at signup asking where people heard about you. It catches the people who didn't click. Read the answers, don't just count them.
  • Timing analysis. Look at the outcome metric around each publish date, against your baseline. A clear lift after a post goes live is evidence, even without a click.
  • Quality signals. Compare creator-referred users to your other channels on the leading indicators you chose. Do they activate faster? Do they stick around longer?

No single method is enough. Together, they give you a picture you can make decisions with. Agree on the mix before launch, too. If you add a signup question after the campaign starts, you lose the earliest responses, which are often the most telling.

Why this improves the campaign itself

Defining success early isn't just about reporting. It changes the work.

When the creator understands the outcome, the content gets sharper. A creator who knows you care about API activation will show the setup flow. One who knows you care about enterprise demos will talk about team workflows. Same product, different content, because the goal was clear.

It also changes creator selection. If your outcome is technical activation, you pick creators whose audiences build things. If it is awareness among executives, you pick different voices on different platforms.

And it keeps everyone honest, including us. A defined outcome means a campaign can clearly miss. That is a feature. It is the only way to learn what works for your product. When we talk about a 3X average ROAS for our clients, the number only means something because return was defined before the money was spent.

Measure what matters

Impressions are easy. GRVTY has delivered more than 100M of them, and they are still the least interesting number in any report we send. Performance Attribution at GRVTY starts by defining what success means to you, then builds measurement around that before a single brief goes out.
