Distribution follows the audience

Most creator campaigns start from a channel template. Better ones start from where buyers already pay attention. Here is how to map distribution.

Salty QaziFounder, GRVTY

Ask most marketing teams for a creator plan and you'll get a channel template. Two YouTube integrations, five posts on X, a LinkedIn push, maybe a podcast if the budget allows. The same plan, more or less, for every product.

Templates feel efficient. They are easy to budget and easy to report on. They are also backwards. They start with the channels and then try to find your audience inside them. Distribution should start with the audience and follow it wherever it already pays attention.

Why templates fail AI companies

AI buyers are not one audience. A machine learning engineer evaluating an inference platform, a product manager looking at a writing assistant, and a CTO deciding whether to standardize on an agent framework all take in information differently.

The engineer might watch long technical walkthroughs on YouTube and follow a few sharp accounts on X. The product manager might read two newsletters and listen to a podcast on the commute. The CTO might be most reachable through LinkedIn and a handful of in-depth newsletters that peers forward along.

A template treats all three the same. It spreads budget evenly across channels, so most of the spend lands where your actual buyer isn't paying attention.

How each channel actually works

Every platform has a different mechanism for building trust. Understanding the mechanism matters more than understanding the reach.

  • YouTube. Long-form, searchable, durable. A good technical walkthrough keeps getting found for months. It is where people go when they want to see a product work before they try it. Best for products that benefit from a demonstration.
  • X. Fast, conversational, reactive. Good for launches and for reaching people who track AI closely every day. Content decays quickly, but the replies often matter as much as the post.
  • LinkedIn. Professional context, decision makers, team-level framing. Better for products where the buyer is a manager or executive, and where the argument is about workflow or business impact.
  • Newsletters. High trust, high attention, small surface area. A reader chose to receive this in their inbox. A recommendation there carries a lot of weight with a focused audience.
  • Podcasts. The deepest attention of any format. Listeners spend long stretches with a host they trust. Good for products with a story that takes time to tell.

None of these is better than the others in general. Each one is better for a specific audience and a specific kind of message.

The question is never which channel is best. It is where your buyer already trusts someone.

A framework for mapping distribution

Here is how we work out where a campaign should run. You can use the same steps.

  1. Name the buyer precisely. Not "developers". Something like backend engineers at companies with a platform team who currently stitch together their own tooling. The more precise you are, the easier every later step gets.
  2. Find where they learn. Ask your best customers where they first heard about tools they now use. Look at what your sales calls reveal. Check which sources your existing users mention when asked.
  3. Find who they trust there. Within each platform, identify the specific voices your buyer follows for AI. This is the slow part, and it is where a vetted creator network earns its keep.
  4. Match format to message. If your product needs a demo, prioritize video. If it needs an argument, prioritize newsletters or LinkedIn. If it needs a story, consider podcasts.
  5. Weight the budget by evidence. Put most of the spend where steps two and three point. Keep a smaller share for testing a second channel you are less sure of.
  6. Sequence, don't scatter. Decide what goes first. A deep video can anchor a campaign that shorter posts on X then point back to.

What changes when you work this way

When distribution follows the audience, a few things happen.

Your budget concentrates. Instead of five thin presences, you have one or two strong ones where your buyer actually is. That matters because trust compounds within a channel. A second mention from a voice the audience already knows is worth more than a first mention somewhere new.

Your content gets better. A creator making content for their own platform, in their own format, produces something native. It doesn't look like a cross-posted ad, so the audience doesn't treat it like one.

Your measurement gets cleaner. With fewer channels and a clear reason for each, it is easier to see what moved and why.

Here is a hypothetical. Say you're launching an observability tool for teams running models in production. A template would put you everywhere. An audience-first plan might find that your buyer reads two specific newsletters, watches a small number of technical YouTube channels, and barely touches LinkedIn. That plan would spend almost nothing on LinkedIn, and the campaign would be stronger for it.

Questions to ask your current plan

Before you approve a creator distribution plan, ask:

  • Which buyer is each channel in this plan meant to reach?
  • What evidence says that buyer pays attention there?
  • Why this format for this message?
  • Which specific creators on each platform does that buyer already trust?
  • What would we cut if the budget were half?
  • Is any channel here because it was in the last plan, rather than because the audience is there?

If the answers are vague, the plan is a template. A good plan should survive every one of these questions with a specific answer, ideally one backed by something your customers actually told you.

Follow the audience
