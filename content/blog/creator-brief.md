Most creator briefs are scripts in disguise. They list talking points, required phrases, a call to action and a few things the creator must not say. Then the brand is surprised when the content sounds like an ad.

The brief is where a campaign is won or lost. A bad brief turns a trusted creator into a spokesperson. A good one gives them enough understanding to say something true and interesting in their own voice. The audience can tell which one they are watching within seconds.

## What a brief is for

A brief has one job: to transfer real product understanding from your team to the creator, fast. Not your positioning. Not your messaging framework. Understanding.

The creators worth working with in AI are technical. They will test your product. They will compare it to what they already use. If the brief gives them slogans instead of substance, they will either ignore it or, worse, follow it and lose their audience's trust in the process.

So every brief we write rests on two foundations: real product understanding and competitive white-space analysis. The first tells the creator what the product actually does. The second tells them where it fits that nothing else does.

## The structure we use

Every brief follows roughly the same structure. You can copy it.

1. **The one-sentence truth.** What does the product do, in plain language, for whom? No adjectives. If you can't write this sentence, you aren't ready to brief anyone.
2. **How it works.** A short, honest explanation of the mechanism. What happens when a user takes the core action? What is the model doing, what is the product doing around it, and where are the limits?
3. **The white space.** What do the closest alternatives do, and where does this product do something different? Be specific and fair. Creators know the alternatives. If you misrepresent them, you lose credibility immediately.
4. **Where it struggles.** What doesn't it do well yet? This section feels risky. It is the most important one. A creator who knows the weak spots can frame them honestly, which makes everything else they say more believable.
5. **What this audience cares about.** Not what you care about. What problem does this creator's specific audience have that the product touches?
6. **Hands-on access.** A working account, a sandbox, sample data, and a technical contact who can answer questions quickly.
7. **The hard constraints.** Legal disclosures, embargo dates, anything genuinely non-negotiable. Keep this short.

Notice what is missing: required talking points, a mandated hook, a scripted call to action. Those things feel like control. In practice they are the parts of a brief most likely to make content sound scripted, and the parts an experienced creator is most likely to quietly ignore.

## Why the weaknesses section matters

Founders push back on section four more than any other. Why would you tell a creator what your product does badly?

Because they will find out anyway. Technical creators test things. If they discover a limitation you didn't mention, they start wondering what else you left out. If you told them up front, it becomes a detail they can handle honestly. A creator who says a tool isn't built for one job, then shows how well it handles another, sounds like someone telling the truth. That is the whole point.

> A brief that hides the weak spots asks the creator to spend their credibility on your behalf. A brief that names them lets the creator protect it.

Audiences trust creators because those creators have been honest before. Your brief should make honesty easy, not risky.

## Give context, not copy

The second most common mistake is writing the content for the creator. It is tempting. You know your product better than anyone. You have spent months on the messaging.

But the creator knows their audience better than you do. They know which formats work, which jokes land, which technical details their viewers will care about and which will lose them. When you hand over copy, you throw that knowledge away.

Here is a hypothetical. Say you're launching an evaluation tool for teams fine-tuning models. Your messaging leads with speed. A creator who runs experiments on their own channel might find that the most interesting thing for their audience is how the tool surfaces regressions between runs. If your brief told them to lead with speed, you would never get that angle. And that angle is the one their viewers would actually share.

Good briefs make room for the creator to find the angle. A feedback loop then catches anything inaccurate before it ships. Feedback should be about facts, not phrasing. Correct a wrong benchmark. Leave the joke alone.

## A checklist before you send it

Before any brief goes out, run it against these questions:

- Could a smart engineer who has never heard of the product understand what it does after reading this?
- Does it describe at least one real limitation?
- Does it name the closest alternatives and describe them fairly?
- Is there anything in it that reads like ad copy? Cut it.
- Does the creator have hands-on access and a fast way to ask questions?
- Is the list of hard constraints short enough that a creator would actually read it?
- Have you said anything about this creator's audience specifically, or is it a generic brief?

If the answer to the last question is no, rewrite it. Briefs should be tailored per creator, even if most of the product section stays the same. The product truth is shared. The audience angle is not.
