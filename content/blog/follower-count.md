The first number anyone looks at when picking a creator is follower count. It sits right on the profile, it is easy to compare, and it feels like a proxy for impact. For AI products, it is close to useless on its own.

Follower count measures one thing: how many accounts clicked follow at some point. It doesn't tell you who those people are, whether they still pay attention, whether they understand the topic, or whether they would act on a recommendation. Those are the things that decide whether a campaign works.

## What follower count hides

Here are the failure modes I see most often when a team picks creators by size.

- **Audience drift.** A creator built their following on one topic and moved to AI recently. Most of their audience followed for something else. Your post reaches them, and they scroll past.
- **Shallow literacy.** The audience is interested in AI as a spectacle. They like demos and predictions. They are not the people who evaluate developer tools or sign procurement forms.
- **Dead weight.** Large accounts accumulate inactive followers over years. The number keeps growing while the share of people who actually see a post shrinks.
- **Spent trust.** Some creators take every sponsorship offered. Their audience has learned that a mention means nothing. You pay for reach and inherit credibility that was already gone.

None of this shows up in the follower count. Some of it only shows up after you have paid.

## The four things we score instead

When we built GRVTY's creator network, we decided early not to rank creators by follower count. Every creator is scored on four dimensions instead. It took months to build, and it is the reason the network is useful.

1. **Technical depth.** Can this person explain how a product works, not just what it does? Do they test things themselves? Do they get the details right when they talk about models, APIs, evals or infrastructure? Technical depth is the entry requirement. Without it, nothing else matters, because the creator can't represent your product accurately.
2. **Audience literacy.** Who is listening, and can they tell good from bad? We read the comments and replies. Are people asking sharp follow-up questions, correcting mistakes, sharing their own results? A literate audience is harder to impress, which is exactly why its attention is worth having.
3. **Engagement quality.** Not engagement rate, which can be inflated by giveaways and controversy. We look at what the engagement says. Substantive replies, saves, people tagging colleagues, people reporting that they tried something. Ten thoughtful replies beat a thousand reactions.
4. **Brand safety.** Does this creator have a history that would embarrass you? Do they disclose sponsorships clearly? Do they make claims they can't back up? In AI, where every claim is already under scrutiny, a creator who overstates things hurts the brands they work with.

Notice that size is not on the list. It is not irrelevant. It just comes after these four, as a tiebreaker.

## Why smaller can beat bigger

Here is a hypothetical. Say you're launching a coding agent for backend teams. You have two options.

Creator A has a huge general tech audience. They cover phones, gadgets and the occasional AI tool. A sponsored video would reach a lot of people.

Creator B has a fraction of that audience. They post detailed walkthroughs of developer tools, run their own comparisons and have a reputation for saying when something doesn't work. Their comments are full of engineers.

Creator A gives you more impressions. Creator B gives you more of the right impressions, delivered with trust attached. For a coding agent, the person who will actually install it and push for a team license is far more likely to be watching Creator B.

> Reach tells you how many people could hear you. Trust tells you how many will listen.

This is what we mean when we say we turn credibility into velocity. Credibility is the input. Velocity, meaning how fast real buyers move from first exposure to trial, is the output. Follower count sits somewhere off to the side.

## Questions to ask before you sign a creator

If you are vetting creators yourself, here is a checklist. You can work through most of it in an hour per creator.

- Read their last twenty posts. What share are about AI? If it is under half, AI is not their beat.
- Find a post where they covered something technical. Did they get it right? Ask an engineer on your team to check.
- Read the replies on their three most recent AI posts. Are people engaging with the substance or just reacting?
- Look for a time they were critical of a product. Creators who never criticize anything have no credibility to lend you.
- Check how they disclose sponsorships. Clear, consistent disclosure is a sign of a creator who protects their audience.
- Ask them how they would approach your product. A creator with real depth will have opinions. If they just ask for talking points, that tells you something.

If a creator passes all six, their follower count matters a lot less. If they fail two or more, a big number should not change your mind.

## Do the work once, use it many times

The honest objection to all of this is time. Scoring a single creator properly takes hours. Scoring dozens, across YouTube, X, LinkedIn, newsletters and podcasts, takes weeks. Keeping those scores current as creators change topics, grow, or burn their audience's trust is ongoing work.

That is why most teams fall back on follower count. It is not that they believe it. It is that it is the only number available on short notice.

The fix is to treat creator evaluation as infrastructure rather than a task you redo for every campaign. Build the scores once, keep them current, and every future campaign starts from a shortlist you already trust.

## The number that actually matters

The right number is not one number. It is a judgment about whether a specific creator's audience overlaps with your buyer, and whether that audience trusts the creator enough to act. That judgment takes work, and it can't be read off a profile.
